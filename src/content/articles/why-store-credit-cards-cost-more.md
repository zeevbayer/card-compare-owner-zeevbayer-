The pitch at checkout is simple: apply for the store card, save 15-20% on today's purchase. What's rarely mentioned at that register is the interest rate attached to the card if you carry any balance on it afterward.

## The rate gap

Retail credit cards carry meaningfully higher interest rates than general-purpose cards on average. The average retail card APR runs above 30%, compared with roughly 21.5% for general-purpose cards. [VERIFY: source URL — CFPB Issue Spotlight on retail credit cards, confirm current figures]

It's not just the average that's higher — the ceiling is too. About 90% of retail cards carry a maximum APR above 30%, compared with 38% of general-purpose cards. [VERIFY: source URL — CFPB Issue Spotlight on retail credit cards]

## Why the approval is easy and the rate is fixed

Many private-label retail cards don't vary the rate much by creditworthiness the way general-purpose cards do. A number of issuers charge close to the same fixed APR to nearly every approved applicant, regardless of credit score. That's part of why approval at the register feels so frictionless — the issuer isn't pricing individual risk the way a bank issuing a general-purpose card typically does. [VERIFY: source URL — CFPB Issue Spotlight on retail credit cards]

## Worked example

You're at checkout with a $200 purchase. Signing up for the store card saves you 15% — $30 off, today.

Say you don't pay the full balance that month and instead carry it, at a 30% APR (near the average for retail cards).

- Discount received: $30
- Balance carried: $170 (after the discount)
- Interest cost at 30% APR, compounding monthly, on a balance paid down gradually

Run the numbers and the $30 discount is wiped out by interest charges in around 13 months of carrying a balance — sooner if you add anything else to the card in the meantime. [VERIFY: source URL — confirm compounding assumptions and payoff schedule used for this estimate]

The discount is real. It's also small compared to what the interest rate can take back if the balance isn't paid off quickly.

## The practical rule

A store card can make sense if you pay the statement in full every month — the discount is free money and the APR never applies. The moment you expect to carry a balance, run the interest cost against the discount before you decide the card is worth it.
