Federal law requires credit card issuers to disclose key rates and fees in a standardized table, commonly called the "Schumer box," named for the legislation that mandated it. [VERIFY: source URL — confirm current Truth in Lending Act / Regulation Z disclosure requirements] It's the one part of a credit card application actually designed to let you compare offers apples-to-apples — most applicants never open it.

Here's what's in it and what to do with each line.

## Purchase APR

The interest rate charged on everyday purchases if you carry a balance past the due date. Often shown as a range (for example, 19.99%–28.99%) because the rate you're offered depends on your creditworthiness at approval. If a card advertises an intro rate, this is the rate that applies once that intro period ends.

**What to do with it:** if you plan to ever carry a balance, this is the single most important number in the box. If you pay in full every month, it's mostly irrelevant to you.

## Penalty APR

A separate, higher APR that can be triggered by specific events — most commonly a late payment. Some issuers apply it after a single late payment; others require multiple. It can apply to your entire balance, not just new purchases, and it can last for a period specified in the cardholder agreement even after you resume paying on time.

**What to do with it:** find out exactly what triggers it and how long it lasts before you need the number, not after.

## Balance transfer fee

A percentage of the amount transferred (commonly 3%–5%), charged when you move a balance from another card onto this one. This is separate from — and stacks with — any interest that applies to the transferred balance once a promotional period ends.

**What to do with it:** weigh this fee against the interest you're actually saving. A 3% fee on a $5,000 transfer is $150 up front, which only makes sense if the promotional rate saves you more than that.

## Cash advance terms

Cash advances (ATM withdrawals, some money transfers, and certain purchase types like casino chips) usually carry their own APR — typically higher than the purchase APR — and start accruing interest immediately, with no grace period. A separate cash advance fee (flat or percentage-based) usually applies on top.

**What to do with it:** treat a credit card cash advance as one of the most expensive ways to access cash. The rate table will show you exactly how expensive.

## What triggers a penalty rate

Beyond the headline APR numbers, look for the specific conditions that move you from your standard rate to the penalty rate — usually one or more late payments within a defined window. This section also typically discloses how long the penalty rate stays in effect once triggered.

**What to do with it:** this is the section most people skip and most regret skipping. It's the difference between a single late payment costing you a fee versus costing you a materially higher rate for months.

## The one habit worth building

Before applying for any card, open the rate table and read purchase APR, penalty APR, and penalty trigger conditions — in that order. Everything else in the marketing is secondary to those three numbers.
