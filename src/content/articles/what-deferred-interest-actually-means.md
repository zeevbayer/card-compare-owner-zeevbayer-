Two credit card offers can both advertise "0% for 24 months" and mean completely different things about what happens if you don't pay the balance off in time.

## The two structures

**True 0% intro APR.** During the promotional period, no interest accrues. If any balance is left when the period ends, interest starts accruing from that point forward, on the remaining balance only. What you paid off already stays paid off.

**Deferred interest.** During the promotional period, interest is still being calculated in the background — you just aren't charged it, conditionally. If you pay the entire original balance in full by the deadline, that interest is waived and you owe nothing extra. But if even a small amount is left unpaid on the last day, the issuer charges you interest retroactively, on the entire original purchase amount, back to day one.

That second structure is what's behind almost every "no interest if paid in full within X months" offer at a retail checkout counter.

## The tell

You can identify which structure you're looking at from the wording alone, before you read a single number in the rate table:

- **"0% intro APR for X months"** → true promotional rate. A remaining balance after the deadline is charged interest only from that point forward.
- **"No interest if paid in full within X months"** → deferred interest. A remaining balance after the deadline triggers interest on the full original amount, retroactive to the purchase date.

## How common is this

Deferred interest is concentrated in store and retail credit cards rather than general-purpose cards. Roughly 80% of store credit cards that offer a 0%-style promotional rate use the deferred interest structure rather than a true intro APR. [VERIFY: source URL — WalletHub store card tracking data]

## Worked example

Say you make a $2,000 purchase on a store card with "no interest if paid in full within 24 months," and the deferred APR that applies if you miss the deadline is 29.99%.

You pay down the balance responsibly for 24 months, but $50 is still outstanding on the deadline.

- **If this were a true 0% intro APR card:** you'd owe interest going forward only on that $50 — a few dollars.
- **Because it's deferred interest:** the issuer calculates interest on the full $2,000 original purchase amount, applied retroactively across the entire 24-month period. At a typical deferred-interest APR, that retroactive charge can run into several hundred dollars — often more than the $50 balance that triggered it.

The $50 left on the table isn't the cost. The two years of interest that was silently accruing the entire time is.

## How to protect yourself

- Read the phrasing before you apply, not just before the deadline.
- If it's a deferred-interest offer, set a reminder at least one full billing cycle before the deadline, not on the deadline itself.
- Paying "most" of the balance does not help — deferred interest is triggered by any remaining balance, not by how large it is.
